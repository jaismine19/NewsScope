## Part 1 — Web scraping: which library?

### The research

I compared two very different approaches to scraping:

* **requests + BeautifulSoup** — download the HTML with `requests` and parse
  the static document with BeautifulSoup. Light, fast, simple, but it only
  sees what the server sends in the first response. If a page builds its
  content with JavaScript, BeautifulSoup sees an empty shell.
* **Playwright** — drives a real headless Chrome browser. It runs the page's
  JavaScript, waits for content to appear, and reads the fully rendered page.
  Far more powerful, but heavier and slower, because a whole browser has to
  run for every page.

### The benchmark

I measured both on the **same 10 NPR article pages** (politely, with a 0.5 s
pause between requests, and Chromium launched once and reused for Playwright):

| Measure | requests + BeautifulSoup | Playwright |
|---------|------------------------:|-----------:|
| Pages fetched | 10 / 10 | 10 / 10 |
| Mean time per page | **0.82 s** | 2.33 s |
| Mean download size | 178 KB | 195 KB |
| Mean extracted text | 5,579 chars | 6,462 chars |

So on a static site like NPR, BeautifulSoup is about **2.8x faster** and both
get the same article text.

### The JavaScript demo (why Playwright still matters)

On `quotes.toscrape.com/js` the quotes only appear after JavaScript runs:

| Library | Quotes found |
|---------|-------------:|
| BeautifulSoup (no JS) | **0** |
| Playwright (runs JS) | **10** |

BeautifulSoup saw nothing — the page is an empty shell until JS executes.
This is the exact situation where a full browser is worth every millisecond.

Chart: `benchmarks/part1_charts.png`.

### My choice

**requests + BeautifulSoup** for this assignment. NPR is a static-HTML site,
so the faster, lighter tool does the job — 2.8x quicker per page with the same
text. Playwright would be my pick the moment a target site renders content
with JavaScript (as the demo shows).

## Part 2 — Scraping 55 articles and analyzing them

### Step 1: the scrape

I collected article links from three NPR sections (technology, business,
science — NPR's section pages ignore `?page=`, so I used three sections to
get enough unique articles), then fetched 55 article pages with
requests + BeautifulSoup and extracted the title, date and body text for
each. To be a good citizen I identified myself with a normal browser
user-agent, waited ~0.6 s between requests, and only visited each public
page once. The raw collection is cached in `data/articles_raw.json`.

### Step 2: four text-analysis algorithms

| Algorithm | What it does | Where it's used |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|
| **LexRank** | graph-based extractive summarization — ranks sentences by how well they represent the article | produces the `summary` column |
| **TextRank** | a second graph-based summarizer | cross-check for LexRank (mean Jaccard agreement: 0.38 — same idea, different sentence picks) |
| **VADER** | rule-based sentiment analysis built for social/news text | gives the direction (positive/negative) and its strength |
| **TF-IDF + cosine** | measures how close each article is to a topic profile of technology/AI keywords | the relevance part of the score |

### The importance score

Each article gets a score from **-100 to +100**:

```
score = direction * (0.5 * relevance + 0.3 * coverage + 0.2 * |sentiment|) * 100
```

* **relevance** — TF-IDF cosine similarity of the article to the
  technology/AI topic profile (0..1).
* **coverage** — how much substance the article has, by length
  (0..1, capped at 800 words).
* **sentiment** — VADER's compound score; its sign is the **direction**
  (positive or negative), its magnitude shows how strongly the article leans.
* Relevance is weighted most (0.5) because the assignment asks for importance
  *in the context of my topic*; coverage and sentiment add the rest.

### The results (tabulated)

The full table is in `data/articles.csv` — one row per article with
`url, title, date, score, direction, sentiment, relevance, summary`.

| Overall | Value |
|---------|-------|
| Articles analyzed | 55 |
| Score range | -86.3 to +98.8 |
| Positive articles | 46 |
| Negative articles | 9 |
| Mean sentiment | 0.65 |
| Mean relevance | 0.28 |
| Mean summary length | ~72 words |

A few honest notes on these numbers:

* Most articles score positive, which mostly reflects the tone of NPR's
  technology coverage in this window (AI hype, successful IPOs, "AI helps
  doctors"). VADER is also known to lean optimistic on news prose, so the
  scores are best read as *relative* — the most negative articles really are
  the lawsuits, crashes and "killer robots" stories.
* The top-scored articles are all squarely on-topic AI stories (Nvidia,
  OpenAI, Anthropic), and the negative tail is led by "California sues
  OpenAI" (-86.3) — exactly what the direction is for.

### What the two summarizers agree on

LexRank and TextRank are cousins, so I used both and compared them. Their
mean Jaccard word-overlap is 0.38: they often pick the same key sentences but
order and framing differ. I published the LexRank summary in the CSV and kept
the TextRank one internally for the cross-check.
